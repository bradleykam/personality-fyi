The Personality Types of US Presidents: We Assessed All 45

Original analysis · Updated 2026-06-17 · By Brad Kam

We assessed every person who has served as US president and assigned each one an MBTI type. One type dominates the office by a wide margin, and the pattern says something uncomfortable about who actually wins the presidency.

The headline: the presidency runs on ISTJ

15 of 45 presidents (33%) are ISTJ, the Logistician - dutiful, disciplined, rule-bound, tradition-first. ISTJ is about 12% of the general population, so a president is roughly 2.7 times more likely to be an ISTJ than a random American is.

The runner-up is the opposite temperament: ESTP, the bold improviser - Andrew Jackson, Theodore Roosevelt, and Donald Trump. The office swings between the steady administrator and the gut-instinct operator, and little else.

Every type, by number of presidents

Three types have never been president

In our assessment, ENTP, INFP, ISFP have never reached the White House. The idealistic INFP and ISFP (values-first, conflict-averse) and the contrarian ENTP (who would rather win the argument than the room) have all been shut out. The presidency rewards a specific kind of duty and decisiveness.

What the pattern shows

  • Steady administrators dominate. The dutiful executive temperament (xSTJ) accounts for a huge share - the country keeps electing managers.
  • The SP types who made it were charismatic, not quiet. The sensing-perceiving presidents were bold ESTP operators (Jackson, TR, Trump). The detached, low-key ISTP basically does not get elected - Grant is the lone exception, and he was handed the office as a war hero.
  • The rare idealists are the memorable ones. The few intuitive-feeling presidents (Lincoln and Wilson as INFJ; Reagan, Clinton, and JFK as ENFP; FDR and Obama as ENFJ) are remembered precisely because they broke the mold.

How we did this (and why you should argue with it)

We assigned each president a type from the documented behavioral record: how they actually made decisions, communicated, and led under pressure, drawn from their speeches, recorded conduct, and biographies. We did not copy popular online typings.

Typing real people is inferential, not clinical, and reasonable people will disagree on individual calls. That is fine. The point is the pattern across 45 data points, not certainty about any single one. If you would type one differently, that is exactly the conversation worth having.
